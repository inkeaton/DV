Chapter 1 — Ideas

Where are the ideas going? From raw volume to emerging themes and their life cycles.

Pick a topic (mock):
Warm-up

How fast did VIS grow?

A 35-year timeline. We start with the simplest signal: volume.

Student lens: notice “recent acceleration” and what that implies for competition & specialization.
Researcher lens: look for phase changes (policy shifts, new tracks, field expansion).
Themes

What are people talking about?

Topics flowing through time. Emerging areas appear as thin bands that thicken.

Pick a topic you’d enjoy for 2–5 years. We’ll carry it through the whole story.
Use this as an “opportunity radar”: growth + cross-track spread often means new collaboration space.
Signal

Emerging, mainstream, niche… or declining?

Growth (recent years) vs total volume (all years): four quadrants, four strategies.

Student strategy: start in “Emerging” or “Niche + growing” for novelty and a clearer PhD pitch.
Researcher strategy: aim for “Emerging + cross-track” to find complementary collaborators.
Reality check

Not everything grows forever

Small multiples show peaks and half-lives: some topics surge, others persist quietly.

Choose a topic with a “long tail” if you want stability; choose a surge if you want novelty.
Watch for “second waves”: mature topics that re-ignite thanks to new methods (e.g., AI).

Chapter 2 — Impact

Impact has many faces: academic attention, real adoption, and community recognition.

Two axes

Adoption vs academic impact

Each dot is a paper. Some are heavily cited, some are heavily used — sometimes both.

Student tip: pick papers with both signals to build a strong foundation quickly.
Researcher tip: “high downloads, medium citations” can indicate under-explored practical value.
Distribution

Does a topic have broad impact… or a few outliers?

Boxplots per topic show whether influence is distributed or concentrated.

Prefer “distributed impact” topics for a safer PhD runway; avoid “one-hit wonder” areas.
Outlier-heavy topics may hide big open problems — but they’re riskier.
Recognition

What does the community reward?

A yearly heatmap: awards and replicability stamps as cultural signals.

Use this to understand “what gets valued” in the field you’re entering.
Look for shifts: new awards / stamps often reflect evolving norms and expectations.

Chapter 3 — People

Science is a network. This chapter turns papers into collaborations, bridges, and teams.

Filters (mock):
Network

Clusters and communities

Nodes are authors, links are coauthorship. Clusters form “labs”, “schools”, and collaborations.

Student goal: find “entry points” — authors who frequently collaborate with newcomers.
Researcher goal: find adjacent clusters for complementary expertise.
Bridges

Who connects worlds?

Ranked “bridge score” (proxy for betweenness). Bridges are often great collaborators.

These are “advisor-like” candidates: they sit at crossroads of multiple communities.
Bridges amplify reach: they can connect your work to other subfields.
Teams

Are papers becoming more collaborative?

Team sizes by year. Collaboration trends shape how you should plan projects.

Expect bigger teams in some areas; build your “collaboration literacy”.
Track differences in team size can hint at method complexity and resource needs.
Mobility

Do people move between tracks?

A simple flow view: which communities exchange authors most.

If your topic is cross-track, you can apply to a wider set of advisors and groups.
Cross-track authors often enable high-impact interdisciplinary work.

Chapter 4 — Places

Ideas travel, but people live somewhere. Where is the talent concentrating — and where is it emerging?

Hubs

Where are the strong groups?

A “bubble map” of institutions. Size reflects activity; accent highlights recent growth.

Student output: a shortlist of 10 groups to explore for your topic.
Researcher output: identify strategic hubs to partner with (especially across regions).
Global

How global is VIS now?

Country activity. Use it to spot emerging regions and shifting centers of gravity.

Practical: consider programs in emerging hubs — they can be less crowded and very strong.
Practical: new regions can be great for growing networks and fresh datasets.
Flows

Where do collaborations happen?

Flows between regions. Strong arcs show frequent coauthorship across borders.

If you want a broad network, aim for regions with many outward connections.
Use this to target “bridge regions” that connect otherwise separate communities.
